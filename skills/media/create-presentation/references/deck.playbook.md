# Plan and verify a deck

Use this guide for a new deck, substantial revision, template adaptation or export. For a small correction, use only the affected checks. Keep the plan and evidence in the project's existing location; a separate report is optional.

## Choose what the audience needs

Capture the brief in a few lines: audience and prior knowledge, intended decision or learning outcome, delivery mode, budget, required source/template, editable target and exports. Separate supported claims from assumptions or proposals. Missing evidence can become a clearly marked gap in a draft; it cannot become invented proof.

Choose the sequence from the requested outcome. These patterns are common starting points; use the actual material rather than forcing one onto every presentation:

| Pattern | Fits | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situation, complication, resolution | Proposals and updates that need a decision | Where things stand, what changed or is at risk, what to do about it |
| Problem, options, recommendation | Decision decks | Criteria first, options compared against them with evidence, then the choice and its cost |
| Before, after, how | Launches, changes and results | The old state, the new state with evidence, the path between them |
| Example, concept, practice | Teaching | A worked case before terminology, the general idea named, then the audience applying it |
| Timeline with turning points | Incident reviews and retrospectives | What happened in order, the decisive moments marked, then causes and actions |

When the audience must decide, state the recommendation early and spend the remaining slides on evidence and trade-offs. When it must first accept a premise, build to the conclusion. End on the decision, ask or next step; a closing slide left up during questions should show that rather than only a thank-you. Preserve required sections in a revision and explain meaningful cuts or reordering.

Map each planned slide to its purpose, supported takeaway, evidence or visual, and any notes or timing it needs. Consecutive slides should advance the argument. Remove repetition or move secondary detail to an appendix when that serves the request. A slide that must survive independent circulation needs enough context to keep its claim accurate.

| Delivery | What must work |
| --- | --- |
| Live talk | The audience can follow while listening. Dense explanation belongs in the delivery or useful speaker notes; visuals and labels remain readable at the actual display size. Budget for demonstrations, transitions and questions where applicable. |
| Asynchronous reading | Readers can understand the argument without a presenter. Include definitions, evidence and limitations in the deck or its accessible appendix. A PDF reader cannot be assumed to receive native speaker notes or animations. |
| Both | Keep one accepted content source, but identify any needed notes or reading version. Do not promise the same export serves both uses when it drops essential explanation. |

Use source facts for chart data and quotations. Check dates, denominators and comparison periods. Distinguish a proposed result from one already achieved. Keep attribution near the claim or in a clearly referenced source section that survives the requested export.

## Make each slide carry one point

Give each content slide one message and write its title as that message in a sentence: "Support tickets fell after the onboarding change" rather than "Support tickets". A topic label leaves the audience to work out the point; a sentence title also shows whether the author has one. If the title cannot fit in one or two lines at display size, the slide probably carries two points. Title, agenda and section-divider slides can keep labels. Michael Alley's [assertion–evidence approach](https://www.assertion-evidence.com/) describes this pattern and its supporting research.

The body is evidence for the title: a chart, screenshot, diagram, example or quotation. Bullets that restate the title add reading without adding support. Check the sequence by reading only the titles in order. They should tell the argument on their own; two titles saying the same thing can merge, and a jump between them shows a missing step.

Set density by delivery mode, extending the table above. A live-talk slide should be absorbable in the few seconds before attention returns to the speaker; most of what is said lives in the delivery and notes. A reading deck can carry complete sentences, qualifications and sources on the slide, organized with short paragraphs and labeled visuals, while still making one point per slide. Move secondary detail to an appendix in either mode.

Make data slides explain. State the finding in the title and show one comparison per chart. Emphasize the series, bar or row carrying the point and mute the rest; annotate the moment that matters, such as the change point or the target, on the chart itself. Round to the precision the decision needs, and keep the unit, period and source visible. A table the audience must scan for the answer needs that row highlighted.

Use builds only when the order of reveal helps: steps of a process the speaker walks through, or an answer that should follow its question. Keep one simple transition style and avoid decorative effects. Each build state should read coherently, and the final state must hold the whole point, because exports and readers may see only one state. Reading decks rarely need builds. Rehearse builds in presenter mode when live use is in scope.

## Reuse a template honestly

Inspect the actual deck before claiming to follow it. Identify page size, master/layout behavior, title and body styles, colors, chart treatment, footers, notes and required assets. A rendered screenshot can show appearance but does not reveal native layout or editability. If the source cannot be opened, state what is inaccessible and avoid claiming an exact adaptation.

Start from a copy when revising a supplied file. Preserve reusable layouts, theme relationships and content outside the requested scope. Keep text as editable text; preserve native shapes, tables and chart data where the target and request require them. An image is appropriate for a photograph or supplied capture, but flattening every slide is not an editable deck. Code-based slide source is acceptable when it is the requested authoring format; it does not stand in for a requested native deck.

Select a small set of layouts suited to the actual content. Use available fonts that survive the intended environment; verify embedding or replacement rather than assuming it. Adjust layout or shorten content before shrinking it into illegibility. Preserve the source's useful voice and necessary caveats when shortening.

For charts, choose a representation that answers the audience's question. Verify data against the source, direct labels, units, meaningful baselines and consistent comparison scales. Do not turn an illustration into a quantitative claim. When an editable chart cannot be preserved, name that limitation before presenting a static image as the requested deliverable.

## Check the final artifacts

Tie checks to the exact saved deck and exports, using a revision, timestamp or hash that distinguishes them from previous outputs. Open the final files rather than inspecting only the authoring code. Keep failures and remaining gaps visible.

| Check | Evidence to inspect |
| --- | --- |
| Content and sequence | Every required section and supported claim appears in the appropriate mode. Titles and order carry the argument; notes-only context has not vanished from a reading export. |
| Every slide | Render/open each final slide at intended viewing size. Check clipping, wrapping, overlaps, alignment, unreadable labels, unexpected font substitution, missing assets and leftovers from the template. Inspect dense slides individually, not just as thumbnails. |
| Charts and tables | Reconcile displayed values with source data. Inspect scales, units, rounding and labels. Confirm required underlying data remains usable, including after target-application import. |
| Accessible structure | Check meaningful slide titles, object reading order, informative visual alt text, decorative treatment, contrast and meaning beyond color. Review link labels and media alternatives. Use the target application's accessibility checker when available, then inspect flagged and material manual criteria. A clean automated report alone does not establish accessibility. |
| Editable target | Open/import the final deck in the intended application. In a disposable copy, test a representative text edit and any required chart-data or object edit, then save and reopen. Check layouts, notes and fonts survived. Record the application/version and exact coverage; success in another renderer leaves target compatibility unverified. |
| Exports | Open each requested format and inspect every page/slide. Verify order, dimensions, page count, text fit, images and links. Check that notes, animations, media or accessibility tags survive where required; document losses rather than assume parity with the native deck. |
| Live use | When required, run slideshow/presenter mode and exercise notes, builds, links and embedded media. Rehearse against the time budget. Report estimated timing separately from an observed run; unavailable audio playback leaves the audio check open. |
| Delivery | Confirm the final file/link is usable through the authorized delivery route. Inspect notes, comments, hidden slides and embedded material for unintended content before external delivery. Local creation does not establish upload, sharing access or publication. |

For PowerPoint, [Microsoft's accessibility guidance](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/accessibility/powerpoint/make-your-powerpoint-presentations-accessible-to-people-with-disabilities) covers titles, reading order, alt text, color and accessible exports. Check current guidance for the actual version when interface details or format behavior matter. Other targets need their own observed checks; the PowerPoint guidance does not prove their compatibility.

If a required tool or application is unavailable, complete accessible work and identify the exact unverified stage. An outline, alternate-format preview or partially checked deck can be useful if labeled accurately; it is not a silent replacement for a requested final file. Do not claim that a renderer viewed slides or that someone listened to media merely because a tool returned success.

## Review the current result

Give an independent agent in fresh context the raw brief, source/template files, accepted constraints and candidate artifacts, with enough access to inspect them. Ask for counterexamples and evidence, without supplying the author's desired verdict. Retain the returned assessment's identity, artifact/revision, observed coverage, findings and gaps. A review of the outline does not cover the rendered deck; a review predating a changed export does not cover that export.

Fix evidenced defects and obtain an independent recheck of the affected content and artifacts. Keep unresolved findings explicit. If the host cannot provide independent review, deliver an accurately labeled unreviewed result within the request's scope. This review is separate from any human decision or authority to share the presentation.
