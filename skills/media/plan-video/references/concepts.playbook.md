# Develop a video concept

Use the sections needed for the requested decision. These are distinct creative steps, not mandatory documents or a fixed production sequence.

## Research: find useful substance

Inspect subject evidence, audience questions and available material before polishing promises. A transcript supports spoken observations; it does not reveal camera work, timing or sound. Record what was actually inspected, its source/date and what remains unknown. For comparisons, include ordinary examples as well as outliers and distinguish a reusable mechanism from a claim that it caused performance.

## Idea: decide what happens

A topic is an area; a concept describes a question, event or experience with an audience reward. “Home gardening” is a topic; showing the same plant under two documented watering schedules is a concept. Its feasibility depends on footage, conditions and results that exist or can be obtained. Compare concepts by relevance, substance, distinctiveness, available proof and effort; avoid invented numerical scores when those inputs are unknown.

Keep each option compact: premise, viewer reward, possible ending, evidence/assets and main constraint. Variants change the presentation of a chosen premise. A request for three concepts needs three different reasons to watch, not three hooks for one premise.

## Format: choose how the reward unfolds

| Format | Useful when | Required material or decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demonstration or tutorial | The viewer needs to see a process and result | Actual steps, visible proof and prerequisites |
| Comparison or experiment | A choice or question can be investigated | Conditions, comparable observations and limits |
| Explanation or case breakdown | A mechanism or supported case needs interpretation | Sources and an application, not only a list of claims |
| Interview or conversation | Perspective emerges from responses | Prompts, consent and room for unscripted answers |
| Documentary or reportage | Events and context carry the piece | Observed versus reconstructed/disputed material |
| Character story, sketch or animation | Actions and consequences deliver emotion or comedy | Feasible action, performance and continuity |
| Essay or reflection | Perspective and reasoning matter | A defensible thread, supporting material and room to think |
| Silent or music-led sequence | Picture, text or sound carries the experience | Visual comprehension, readable holds and sound rights |

Animation is a production method; vertical is a layout. Neither settles the narrative. A character, narrator, rapid edit or talking head should serve this piece rather than appear by default. Long form needs a meaningful progression and chapters when useful; it is not an extended short with more teasers.

## Angle: make the contribution concrete

Name what this piece adds: a demonstrated result, useful explanation, perspective, lived experience or new connection. A supported disagreement can work; a contrarian claim is not required. If the outline offers no substance beyond a familiar list, improve the research or change the idea before selling it harder.

## Packaging: set an honest expectation

For browse-driven work, draft a title and thumbnail concept. For a feed, consider the first frame and visible opening. State the expectation they create and where the piece delivers it. Distinguish a planned visual from an available asset; do not imply a staged or hypothetical result was observed. Revise packaging when the evidence or ending changes.

## Series and batches: repeat the promise, vary the story

A series needs a recognizable audience reward and enough different premises to sustain it. Reuse character/set references only when continuity matters. Choose a small pilot whose assets, permissions, production effort and review time fit the brief. Mark shared assets and dependencies, keep concepts separate from visual or hook variants, and avoid multiplying combinations into an unauthorized batch.

After publication, use real comparable observations to choose a next experiment: metric definition, date/window, denominator, distribution conditions and actual cost where known. Unknown cost is not zero. A different topic, distribution or measurement window limits causal conclusions; editorial preference is not demonstrated audience impact.
